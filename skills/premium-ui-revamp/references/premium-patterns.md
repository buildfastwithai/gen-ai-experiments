# Premium Patterns and Anti-Patterns

Use this reference to replace generic visual decisions with product-specific ones. Select a coherent subset; do not combine all patterns.

## Choose a direction from context

### Precision tool

Fit analytics, developer, finance, operations, and productivity products.

- Use a disciplined grid, compact rhythm, clear data alignment, and restrained color.
- Emphasize state, comparison, keyboard access, and information density.
- Create character through typography, dividers, data visualization, or a distinctive navigation model rather than decoration.

### Editorial clarity

Fit publishing, research, education, portfolios, and content-rich products.

- Use strong type hierarchy, generous but purposeful margins, readable measures, and image pacing.
- Let content create the rhythm.
- Create character through scale contrast, captions, rules, and art direction.

### Quiet confidence

Fit professional services, premium commerce, health, finance, and high-trust products.

- Use controlled neutrals, precise spacing, concise copy, and excellent photography or product detail when available.
- Favor subtle surface changes over obvious gradients and shadows.
- Create character through proportion, material cues, and restraint.

### Warm utility

Fit community, wellness, education, consumer productivity, and human-centered services.

- Use approachable type, softer geometry, comfortable rhythm, and a warm accent.
- Keep controls direct and useful; avoid turning friendliness into cartoon decoration.
- Create character through language, illustration, and tactile interaction details.

### Expressive brand

Fit culture, entertainment, creative tools, launches, and campaigns.

- Use a bold composition, distinctive type or imagery, and deliberate motion.
- Keep utility areas calmer so expressive moments retain impact.
- Create character through one memorable motif rather than a pile of effects.

## Replace common generic choices

### Card grids everywhere

Problem: every idea becomes a rounded rectangle, flattening hierarchy and wasting space.

Replace with section rhythm, dividers, aligned lists, split layouts, tables, or a single dominant panel. Use a card only when the item is independently selectable, movable, or meaningfully self-contained.

### Gradient headline plus vague subtitle

Problem: visual drama substitutes for a concrete promise.

Replace with specific copy, decisive type, product evidence, and a composition tied to the actual task. Keep a gradient only when it belongs to the brand system and remains readable.

### Decorative dashboard chrome

Problem: glowing charts, glass surfaces, and saturated indicators compete with the data.

Replace with strong number formatting, comparison context, aligned scales, useful annotations, and color reserved for meaning.

### Excessive rounding

Problem: pills and large radii make unrelated controls look identical and reduce visual precision.

Replace with a radius scale tied to component size and role. Reserve pills for tags, filters, compact status, or genuinely capsule-shaped controls.

### Too many floating surfaces

Problem: shadows and elevation appear without a spatial model.

Replace with borders, background shifts, spacing, or containment. Use elevation for overlays, menus, dragged objects, or a genuinely raised focal element.

### Arbitrary accent color

Problem: default blue or purple appears on headings, icons, buttons, and backgrounds without semantics.

Replace with a restrained accent policy. Define where accent color means action, selection, status, or brand expression, and keep neutrals responsible for most hierarchy.

### Icon-in-a-circle repetition

Problem: repeated medallions add noise and make every item equally important.

Replace with well-aligned standalone icons, typographic markers, numbers, illustrations, or no icon. Add a container only when it communicates state or interaction.

### Empty luxury

Problem: extreme whitespace and tiny copy imitate luxury while weakening usability.

Replace with confident proportions, high-quality type, concise content, and intentional negative space around genuinely important material.

### Animation as decoration

Problem: every element fades, floats, or staggers on entry, delaying access and making utility screens feel theatrical.

Replace with motion that explains navigation, state change, hierarchy, or direct manipulation. Keep the rest immediate.

## Add distinction without clutter

Choose at most one or two techniques that fit the product:

- a recognizable grid or asymmetric composition;
- a distinctive but readable display type treatment;
- a branded rule, corner, underline, or crop behavior;
- a consistent image aspect ratio and art direction;
- a purposeful data-visualization language;
- a subtle material treatment tied to the product domain;
- concise, unmistakably product-specific copy;
- one meaningful transition used at a key moment.

Repeat the chosen idea consistently enough to feel intentional. Stop before it becomes a theme effect.

## Preserve credibility

- Prefer real product screenshots, data, and states over abstract mockups.
- Keep marketing claims concrete and support them with evidence.
- Use familiar interaction patterns for high-stakes actions.
- Avoid adding testimonials, metrics, customer logos, security badges, or guarantees that the product does not actually have.
- Keep implementation quality aligned with the visual promise: responsive behavior, loading states, focus states, and error handling are part of the premium finish.
